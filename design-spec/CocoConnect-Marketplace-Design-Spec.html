A1 — What this document is

Context Read first

The brief in one paragraph

CocoConnect is the trading arm of Coconoto — a marketplace where farmers, cooperatives and processors list coconut products and buyers across the value chain source them. It works today, but it was built straight from component primitives with no design pass. Pages are dense, the product detail step is a modal instead of a page, and three screens that a marketplace cannot do without — a product page, a vendor storefront and an order confirmation — do not exist. This document is the redesign brief: it fixes the information architecture and says what has to be drawn. It deliberately does not prescribe the visual language; that is the designer's job.

Who the product serves

Buyers

Businesses sourcing coconut inputs — food and beverage processors, cosmetic and coir manufacturers, aggregators, exporters and institutional buyers. They compare price per unit, available volume, quality grade and lead time. They are not impulse shoppers.

Sellers

Farmers, cooperatives and processors with produce to move. Many will be on a phone, often on poor connectivity, and are not confident with dashboards. Listing a product has to feel like filling a simple form, not operating software.

What exists today

ScreenRouteCondition
Marketplace browse/cococonnectWorks. Search, category rail, sort, grid. Visually untreated.
Order modal— (in-page)Carries product info, quantity and checkout in one dialog. No URL, no share.
Buyer login / signup/buyer-login, /buyer-signupBoth work. Login has a designed split panel; signup is an unstyled form card.
Buyer dashboard/buyer-dashboardOrders, profile, upgrade-to-seller. Reasonably built; no overview landing.
Seller dashboard/vendor-dashboardProducts / orders / profile tabs. Two separate implementations exist.
Seller login / signup/vendor-login, /vendor-signupWork. Login is designed, signup is not.
Not built at all—No product page, no vendor storefront, no basket, no order confirmation.

Guiding principles

  • One clear next action per screen. Every page answers "what do I do here?" without the user hunting.
  • Trust over gloss. B2B buyers want verification, minimum order, lead time and grade — not lifestyle photography. Consumer-marketplace gloss will read as unserious.
  • Say "request", not "buy". There is no payment on the platform; a vendor confirms the order by hand. Every label, button and confirmation must reflect that so nobody expects instant checkout.
  • Mobile is the seller's primary device. Design the seller side at 375 px first. The buyer side can be desktop-led.
  • Price and unit never separate. ₦4,500/kg is one object; never let a line break split them.
  • Nothing that deserves a URL hides in a modal. Product detail, checkout and confirmation all become pages.

Priority tiers

P0 — must ship the eight screens a buyer or seller cannot complete a task without. P1 — should ship needed for a credible B2B marketplace. P2 — reserve acknowledged, not designed in this round.

A2 — Design system reference

Context Current state — designer may replace

These are the tokens in use across the site today. The redesign is free to revisit them, but anything kept should be kept deliberately — the marketplace shares a navbar and footer with the rest of the site, so a divergent palette will look pasted on.

Colour

HexRole todayNotes

#8CC63FLime accentActive nav underline, small highlights. Sparingly.

#1f1611Footer backgroundNear-black brown. Anything on it needs real contrast.

Emerald / amber / roseSemanticIn-stock, low-stock, out-of-stock and order-status chips. Keep as a defined set, not ad-hoc classes.
Ask. The marketplace currently runs a glassmorphism treatment — frosted white cards over a radial green gradient, with blur orbs. It is decorative and reads consumer. For a B2B trading surface, a flat light background with a denser grid and heavier price typography will communicate more.

Typography & spacing

  • Headings: Lora (serif) site-wide; marketplace uses a geometric sans. Pick one for the marketplace and state it.
  • Body: system sans, 14–16 px.
  • Numbers: prices and quantities need a tabular figure so columns line up.
  • 4 px base unit. Card padding 16/20/24.
  • Radius: 8 px controls, 12–16 px cards.
  • Shadow: one soft elevation only. No multi-layer glow.

Layout & breakpoints

A3 — Global elements

All screens Redesign

These appear on most or all screens. Design them once, in all states, before the individual pages — every page spec below assumes them.

Navbar

  • Sticky, 72 px tall, stays in document flow (no compensating page padding).
  • Left: Coconoto wordmark. Centre: Home · Services (dropdown: Coco-Tech, CocoCycle Hub, Coco-Connect, Coco Drink & Eat) · Marketplace · Blog · About Us · Contact us.
  • Right: the account cluster — this is the one region that differs per screen.
  • Under 1024 px: logo + hamburger; nav becomes a full-height sheet with the account links at the bottom.
  • Active item is derived from the route, so Marketplace lights up on every /cococonnect/* page.

Account cluster — three states

StateShows
Signed outSingle green "Login" pill.
Signed inInitials avatar + chevron. Dropdown: Profile · My Orders · Sell on Coconoto · Sign out.
Signed in + sellerSame, plus "Seller dashboard" as the first item.
Gap to fix. There is no basket indicator anywhere in the navbar. Once the basket exists (B4), the cluster needs a basket icon with an item count between the search and the avatar.

Footer

  • Shared site footer, dark brown #1f1611 background, green logo mark, link columns.
  • Identical on every marketplace page — do not redesign per page.

Repeating patterns

  • Empty state. Centred icon in a soft tinted square, one bold line, one explanatory line, one action button. Every list on the site uses this shape.
  • Loading. Skeleton cards that match the real card's proportions — never a spinner on a list.
  • Error. Inline bordered strip above the content with a retry action. Full-page error only when nothing at all loaded.
  • Toast. Bottom-right, auto-dismiss, success/error variants.

B1

Marketplace Browse

/cococonnect Redesign Public P0

Purpose

The shop floor. A buyer lands here from search or the nav, narrows to what they need in as few moves as possible, and opens a product.

Content, top to bottom

  1. NavbarWith the account cluster and (new) basket indicator.
  2. Hero band + searchHeadline "Discover Quality Coconut Products", one-line subhead, three trust badges — Verified vendors · Nationwide delivery · Quality guaranteed — and the full-width search field. Search covers product name, description and vendor, with a clear (✕) when filled. Keep the band shallow: it is a signpost, not a billboard.
  3. Category navigationDesktop: a 270 px sticky rail with "All Products" plus the 11 categories, each with a live count. Mobile: a row of the top 4–5 categories as quick-chips, plus a "Categories" button opening a right-hand drawer with the same items.
  4. Results toolbar"Showing N products", the active filter as a removable chip, Sort (Newest · Price low→high · Price high→low · Name A–Z), and a grid/list toggle.
  5. Product grid2 columns mobile, 3 desktop. Card: image, category badge, name (2-line clamp), vendor name + verified tick, price with unit, stock chip. Whole card is the link target.
  6. Load more + how it worksNew. The grid currently renders everything at once — pick a page size and one pattern. Below it, a three-step strip: Browse → Send a request → Vendor confirms, which sets the expectation that this is an enquiry marketplace.
  7. FooterShared site footer.

States to design

  • Loading — six skeleton cards in the real grid proportions.
  • No results — search term echoed back, category named if active, "Clear filters" action.
  • Empty category — "No products in this category yet" plus a route back to All Products.
  • Load failure — inline strip with Retry. Plus the mobile drawer open and closed.
Judgement call. The page is currently one frosted card floating on a green gradient with decorative blur orbs — the biggest single thing making the marketplace look like a D2C shop. A flat surface, a tighter card and a larger price will do more for credibility than any new component.
Decide. Show seller location and minimum order on the card itself? For B2B sourcing this is often the deciding factor, and it costs one line.

B2

Product Detail

/cococonnect/product/:id New page Public P0

Purpose

Give a product a home of its own so it can be shared, indexed and described properly. Today the only way to see a product is the order modal, which has no URL and no room for the specifications a B2B buyer needs before enquiring.

Content, top to bottom

  1. BreadcrumbMarketplace / {Category} / {Product name}. Doubles as a route back to the filtered grid.
  2. GalleryMain image at a fixed ratio with thumbnails beneath. Products currently carry a single image, so design for one and leave room for four. Missing image falls back to the coconut placeholder.
  3. Buy panelRight of the gallery. Product name, vendor line with the verified tick (links to B3), price with unit, stock status, quantity stepper with the available maximum, and the primary action.
  4. Primary + secondary actionsPrimary: "Request to order". Secondary: "Add to basket" and "Message vendor". Out of stock replaces the primary with "Notify me when available".
  5. Specification blockCategory · Unit of measure · Available stock · Minimum order · Packaging · Lead time · Origin. The italicised four are new fields the designer should lay out now even if they ship later — they are the reason a B2B buyer trusts a listing.
  6. Vendor cardAvatar or logo, business name, verified badge, location, member-since, and "View storefront". This is the trust anchor of the page.
  7. Full descriptionLong-form text. Design for up to ~800 characters with a "read more" clamp.
  8. Similar productsThree or four cards from the same category, reusing the grid card.
  9. FooterShared site footer.

States to design

  • Loading — gallery and panel skeletons.
  • Out of stock — CTA disabled, "Notify me" replacement, chip on the gallery.
  • Product not found — a proper 404 within the marketplace chrome, not the global error page.
  • No image — the coconut placeholder at both gallery and thumbnail size.
Why this is P0. Product pages are what search engines index and what buyers paste into WhatsApp when asking a colleague. Without it the marketplace cannot be found or shared.

B3

Vendor Storefront

/cococonnect/vendor/:id New page Public P1

Purpose

Let a buyer see who they are dealing with, and everything that seller offers, in one place. In B2B sourcing the seller matters as much as the product — buyers consolidate with one supplier. Today a vendor is a single line of text under a product name.

Content, top to bottom

  1. BreadcrumbMarketplace / {Business name}.
  2. Storefront headerLogo or initials avatar, business name, verified badge, location, member since. If a banner image is ever added, this is where it goes.
  3. DescriptionThe vendor's own words about the business, clamped to three lines with an expand.
  4. Stat stripProducts listed · Categories covered · Orders fulfilled (if we choose to show it) · Member since. Three or four compact stat cards.
  5. Contact blockPhone and email as tap-to-act chips. Buyer-side only; never expose more than the vendor consented to publish.
  6. Filter + sort rowWithin-store category filter and the same sort options as the main grid.
  7. Product gridThe vendor's active products, reusing the B1 card.
  8. FooterShared site footer.

States to design

  • Vendor with no active products — storefront still renders, grid shows "This seller has no active listings right now".
  • Unverified vendor — no tick; do not draw attention to the absence, but never show a "pending" badge publicly.
  • No logo — initials avatar fallback.
  • Vendor not found — in-chrome 404.
Decide. Should a vendor's phone number be public, or only revealed after the buyer sends a request? Public is better for conversion; gated is better for spam control. This is a business call, not a design one — but the design changes depending on the answer.

Request Basket

/cococonnect/basket New page Buyer P1

Purpose

Let a buyer enquire about more than one product at a time. Today each product has to be ordered through its own form, so a buyer sourcing three items fills three forms and the vendor receives three unrelated orders.

Content, top to bottom

  1. Page title + count"Your request basket" with the number of items and distinct vendors.
  2. Line itemsGrouped by vendor. Each line: image, product name (links back to B2), unit price with unit, quantity stepper, line total, remove. Quantity changes recalculate live.
  3. Per-vendor subtotalBecause fulfilment is per vendor, each group needs its own subtotal, not just one grand total.
  4. Order summarySticky on desktop, at the bottom on mobile. Subtotal, delivery shown as "Quoted by vendor", total. One line of plain-language explanation that no payment is taken now.
  5. Notes fieldOptional free text applied to the whole request.
  6. Primary action"Continue to request" — deliberately not "Checkout".
  7. Empty stateBasket icon, "Nothing here yet", "Browse the marketplace" action.

States to design

  • Empty basket — the most likely first view; make it inviting, not apologetic.
  • Item went out of stock while in the basket — inline warning on that line, quantity capped, remove offered.
  • Item's vendor deactivated it — line struck through with a reason.
  • Multi-vendor basket — two or three vendor groups stacked.
  • Quantity above available stock — stepper clamps and shows "Max N".
Decide. Is a basket the right model for a quote-based marketplace? Recommendation: yes, because the alternative is a worse experience, not a simpler one — but rename it "request basket" everywhere and keep every label in request language so no one looks for a payment step that does not exist.
Open question. Multi-vendor requests currently have no backend concept — one order row points at one vendor. If the basket can hold two vendors, that has to be resolved before build. Flag this early.

Place Order

/cococonnect/checkout Redesign Buyer + guest P0

Purpose

Capture the buyer's details and delivery information and hand the request to the vendor. Today this is a modal on top of the product grid doing three jobs at once. It becomes a page; the product information moves out to B2 and B4.

Content, top to bottom

  1. Progress indicatorTwo steps — Details → Confirm. Even a minimal indicator reduces abandonment on a form this long.
  2. Buyer detailsFull name, email, phone. Pre-filled and read-only-friendly when the buyer is signed in; editable when they are a guest.
  3. DeliveryDelivery address (multi-line), city, state, country. Signed-in buyers get their saved address pre-filled from their profile.
  4. NotesFree text — "any special requests". Keep it, it is used.
  5. Guest save-time panelWhen signed out: a compact bordered panel offering Login / Create account, explaining that an account saves details and tracks orders. Non-blocking — a guest must still be able to submit.
  6. Order summarySticky right column: line items, quantity, subtotal, "Delivery quoted by vendor", total. Every item links back to its product page.
  7. Submit"Send request to vendor". Disabled while submitting, with a spinner state.
  8. Legal lineOne short line: the vendor will be notified and will contact you to confirm details and payment. Already present in the modal — keep it, it is doing real work.

States to design

  • Guest — empty fields, save-time panel visible.
  • Signed in — fields pre-filled from the profile, panel replaced by a "signed in as…" line with a change option.
  • Validation errors — per-field, on blur and on submit.
  • Quantity exceeds stock — inline error, submit blocked.
  • Submitting — button busy, form locked, no double submit.
  • Submit failed — error strip above the form, everything the buyer typed preserved.
Keep. The quantity stepper clamping to available stock, and the live total recalculation. Both already work and both are the kind of detail that erodes trust when missing.

B6

Order Confirmation

/cococonnect/order/:reference New page Buyer + guest P1

Purpose

Close the loop. Right now success is a dialog that auto-dismisses after two seconds, leaving the buyer with no reference number, no record and nothing to screenshot. Every order needs a permanent, linkable confirmation.

Content, top to bottom

  1. Success headerLarge success mark, "Request sent", and the buyer's email echoed back so they know where to look.
  2. Reference numberNew. A short human-readable reference per order, displayed prominently and copyable. This is the single most requested thing after an order is placed.
  3. What happens nextThree numbered steps — the vendor reviews your request → confirms price, quantity and delivery → you receive a confirmation email and arrange payment directly. This is where the no-payment model is explained for the last time.
  4. Order summaryRead-only recap: items, quantities, unit prices, subtotal, delivery, total, delivery address, notes.
  5. Vendor contactBusiness name with email and phone as action chips, so the buyer can chase immediately if needed.
  6. Next actions"View my orders" (or "Create an account to track this" for guests), and "Continue browsing".
  7. FooterShared site footer.

States to design

  • Guest confirmation — extra emphasis on creating an account so the order is trackable.
  • Signed-in confirmation — "View my orders" leads straight to the order in B9.
  • Multi-vendor request — one confirmation per vendor, or one page listing each vendor's section. Decide and design accordingly.
  • Reference not found — in-chrome 404 with a route back to the marketplace.
  • Email not delivered — a soft warning with a resend action (P2, but reserve the slot).
Why it matters more than it looks. A confirmation page is the artifact buyers screenshot and forward internally to get sign-off. For a B2B marketplace that is the moment a request turns into a real purchase.

B7

Buyer Login

/buyer-login Redesign Public P0

Purpose

Sign an existing buyer in, quickly, with an obvious exit to the marketplace. The split-panel pattern already exists on the seller login — reuse it so the two feel like siblings.

Content, top to bottom

  1. Slim brand barLogo left, "Home" action right. Deliberately not the full site navbar — a login page should not invite the user to wander off.
  2. Left panel (desktop only)Dark brand panel with a short pitch and three benefit rows: order tracking, saved delivery details, direct vendor contact. Include the panel on ≥1024 px only; below that it collapses away.
  3. Form cardTitle "Buyer sign in", one line of supporting copy.
  4. Email fieldWith icon, validation on blur, error styling and message.
  5. Password fieldWith icon, show/hide toggle, "Forgot password?" aligned to the label.
  6. Remember meCheckbox, default on — it remembers the email, not the session. Label it honestly ("Remember my email").
  7. SubmitFull width, primary green, busy state with spinner.
  8. Alternate paths"New here? Create account" and a clear "Selling instead? Seller login" link, plus "Back to marketplace".

States to design

  • Idle — empty, autofocused email.
  • Field invalid — red border, helper text, focus ring.
  • Wrong credentials — alert block above the form, fields preserved.
  • Submitting — button busy, inputs locked.
  • Account disabled — a distinct message with a support route.
  • Post-signup arrival — a success strip confirming the account was created.
Missing today. "Forgot password?" links to a query parameter that has no screen behind it. Either design a reset flow or remove the link — a dead password-reset link is worse than none.

Interaction states every control needs

Buttons & links

Default · hover · active · focus-visible · disabled · busy. The focus ring must be visible — it is a keyboard-accessibility requirement, not a nicety.

Form fields

Default · focus · filled · invalid · disabled · read-only. Error text sits below the field and is announced to screen readers.

Cards & rows

Default · hover (pointer only) · focus · selected · disabled. Cards that are clickable must be reachable by keyboard.

Accessibility floor

  • Body text at 4.5:1 contrast minimum; the green on white and the muted grey both need checking.
  • Never convey stock or order status by colour alone — pair every chip with a word or icon.
  • Every image needs alt text; product images fall back to the product name.
  • Touch targets at least 44 × 44 px, especially the quantity steppers and the mobile drawer close button.
  • All flows must be completable with the keyboard alone, including the product grid and the order status control.

D2 — Handoff checklist

Delivery

What to deliver, per screen

  • Three frames minimum — desktop 1440, tablet 768, mobile 375. Seller screens: mobile is the primary, so design that one first.
  • Every state from the D1 matrix, drawn on the artboard, not described in a comment.
  • Interaction states for every interactive element — hover, focus, disabled, busy.
  • Empty and error states for every list and form.
  • Final copy in the frames. No lorem ipsum, no "TBD". If a string is not decided, raise it as an open question instead.
  • Spacing annotations on at least one representative frame per screen.
  • Assets exported at 2×, named as below.

File & layer naming

  • Frames: B1 Marketplace — Desktop 1440, C3 Seller Dashboard — Mobile 375.
  • Components: ProductCard, CategoryRailItem, QuantityStepper, StockChip, VerifiedBadge, OrderStatusChip, EmptyState.
  • Icons: use one set throughout — the build uses Lucide, so staying on Lucide avoids a redraw.
  • Exports: product-placeholder@2x.png, avatar-fallback@2x.svg, hero-illustration@2x.webp.

Assets needed from Coconoto

  • Wordmark files at the correct ratios — the current assets are 760 × 121 and 2170 × 725, and the footer needs the green mark rather than the brown one.
  • A coconut product placeholder illustration for missing images.
  • Three or four real product photographs for the demo grid, including one on a white background.
  • A seller logo and a buyer avatar for the demo states.

Open decisions

Each of these changes the design. They need an answer before or during the first review, not after.
